Where this sits: flow theory and the Safe System

  • Pedestrian flow theory treats pedestrians as a traffic flow with its own fundamental diagram
  • WHO Pedestrian Safety manual (2023): a Safe System approach for decision-makers
  • GDCI and GIZ SUTP: design guidance for walking and cycling in fast-growing cities
  • Comfort and safety are two separate tests — a route must pass both

3 / 24

Pedestrian flow theory treats pedestrians as a traffic flow, with its own fundamental diagram linking speed, density and flow. The WHO Pedestrian Safety manual (2023) sets out a Safe System approach for decision-makers. GDCI and GIZ SUTP give design guidance for walking and cycling in fast-growing cities. A route must pass two separate tests, comfort and safety.

Pedestrian flow: the fundamental diagram

Schematic after pedestrian flow theory: speed falls with density; flow peaks at capacity, then collapses.

4 / 24

The left panel shows that walking speed falls as the footway fills with people, measured as pedestrians per square metre. The right panel shows that flow rises with density until it peaks at capacity, and then collapses. In the congested part, queues form at crossings and stairs. The diagram is a schematic, and it follows the same logic as vehicle traffic in Week 10.

What the flow theory means for design

  • Flow = density × speed; capacity is reached long before people stop moving
  • Bottlenecks decide capacity: a crossing island or metro stair, not the footway
  • People self-organise into lanes in two-way flow — until obstacles break the lanes
  • Design for comfort (space per person), not for capacity
  • Measure peak 15-minute flows at the narrowest point

5 / 24

Flow equals density times speed, and capacity is reached long before people stop moving. The capacity of a route is set by its bottlenecks, such as a crossing island or a metro stair, not by the open footway. In two-way flow, people form lanes by themselves until obstacles break them up. So design for comfort, meaning enough space per person, and measure the busiest 15-minute flow at the narrowest point.

Worked example: how wide should the footway be?

  • Peak flow 1,800 pedestrians per hour; comfortable flow 1,200 per hour per metre
  • Clear path needed = 1,800 ÷ 1,200 = 1.5 m
  • Add 1.0 m for kerb, poles, trees and shop fronts → 2.5 m total
  • A parked car on the footway removes about 2 m — the path disappears
  • Check the narrowest point, not the average

6 / 24

This worked example sizes a footway from its flow. A peak of 1,800 pedestrians per hour, at a comfortable 1,200 per hour per metre, needs a clear path of 1,800 ÷ 1,200 = 1.5 m. Adding 1.0 m for the kerb, poles, trees and shop fronts gives 2.5 m in total. A car parked on the footway takes about 2 m, so the clear path disappears. Check the narrowest point, not the average width.

Universal access

  • Step-free routes: kerb ramps at every crossing, no steps into underpasses
  • Tactile paving and audible signals for blind and partially sighted people
  • Enough crossing time for slow walkers; resting places and shade
  • Pulatova's survey: 73.1% see no infrastructure for people with disabilities
  • OSM: 4 of 4,846 crossings (73 tagged) have tactile paving recorded — mostly unmapped

7 / 24

Universal access means routes that everyone can use, including people with disabilities. That requires step-free routes with kerb ramps, tactile paving and audible signals for blind people, enough crossing time for slow walkers, and places to rest in the shade. In Pulatova's survey, 73.1% see no infrastructure for people with disabilities. OpenStreetMap records tactile paving at 4 of 4,846 crossings (73 tagged), but a missing tag does not prove that the paving is missing.

The Safe System: speed is the master variable

  • People make mistakes; the system should stop mistakes from killing them
  • The energy in a crash rises with the square of speed
  • Where people walk and cross, keep motor-vehicle speed low
  • Where speed must be high, separate people in space (barriers) or time (signals)
  • Lighting, visibility and crossing distance complete the system

8 / 24

The Safe System accepts that people make mistakes and asks that a mistake should not kill anyone. Speed matters most, because the energy in a crash rises with the square of speed. Where people walk and cross, motor traffic should be slow. Where speed must stay high, people are separated in space by barriers or in time by signals. Lighting, visibility and short crossings complete the system.

Speed and stopping distance

Illustrative physics: 1.5 s reaction, 7 m/s² braking on a dry road. At 60 km/h a driver needs about 45 m to stop.

9 / 24

The chart shows how far a car travels before it stops, at four speeds. The distance has two parts: the reaction time of 1.5 s, and braking at 7 m/s² on a dry road. Both parts grow with speed, and braking grows fastest, with the square of speed. At 60 km/h a driver needs about 45 m to stop, which is why speed is so dangerous at crossings.

Designing crossings

  • Put crossings on desire lines: at stops, schools, metro exits, shop clusters
  • Shorten exposure: kerb extensions, refuge islands, fewer lanes to cross
  • Keep sightlines clear: no parking just before the crossing
  • Signals: short waits, enough green for slow walkers
  • Raised crossings and tables slow cars where signals are not justified

10 / 24

Put crossings on desire lines, the routes people want to walk, such as at stops, schools, metro exits and groups of shops. Shorten the time people are exposed to traffic with kerb extensions, refuge islands and fewer lanes to cross. Keep sightlines clear by banning parking just before the crossing. Signals need short waits and enough green for slow walkers, and raised crossings slow cars where signals are not justified.

Worked example: crossing time for an older pedestrian

  • Four lanes × 3.5 m = 14 m of carriageway
  • At 1.2 m/s: 14 ÷ 1.2 ≈ 12 s; at 0.9 m/s (older walker): ≈ 16 s
  • A central refuge splits it into two crossings of 7 m (about 8 s each)
  • Signal green for pedestrians must cover the slow walker, not the average

11 / 24

This worked example checks crossing time for an older person. Four lanes of 3.5 m make 14 m of carriageway. Crossing it takes about 12 s at 1.2 m/s, but about 16 s at 0.9 m/s, the speed of an older walker. A central refuge splits it into two crossings of 7 m, about 8 s each. The pedestrian green must cover the slow walker, not the average one.

Traffic-calming toolbox

MeasureWhat it doesWhere it fits
Lane narrowing / road dietLowers speed, shortens crossingsWide arterials with spare lanes
Raised crossing or tableForces low speed at the crossingLocal and collector streets, school entrances
Refuge islandTwo-stage crossingMulti-lane roads
Kerb extensionShorter crossing, better visibilityStreets with kerbside parking
Area-wide low speed limitLower speed everywhereMahalla streets, school zones

12 / 24

Traffic calming means changing the street so that drivers slow down. The table lists five tools, what each does and where it fits. A road diet narrows lanes or removes them, which lowers speed and shortens crossings on wide arterials. Raised crossings suit local streets and school entrances, refuge islands suit multi-lane roads, and kerb extensions suit streets with kerbside parking. An area-wide low speed limit fits mahalla streets and school zones.

Tashkent: who gets hurt, and where

  • TIP manuscript (Sodikov et al., under review), Pulatova (2024), OpenStreetMap October 2026

13 / 24

The second part of the lecture asks who gets hurt on Tashkent's streets, and where. It uses a crash study by Sodikov and colleagues that is under review, Pulatova's (2024) survey, and OpenStreetMap data from October 2026.

Pedestrians and the night carry the risk

  • 57.4% of 2016 police crash records (August excluded) were pedestrian strikes
  • Recorded death: 3.46% vs 1.08% (pedestrians vs vehicle occupants)
  • Overnight: 8.13% vs 1.90%; adjusted OR 5.99 (3.91–9.19)
  • Model prediction, older pedestrian at night: 26.7% vs 8.6% for an occupant

14 / 24

In the 2016 police crash records, leaving out August, 57.4% were pedestrian strikes. Recorded death was 3.46% for pedestrians against 1.08% for vehicle occupants, and 8.13% for overnight crashes against 1.90% for daytime ones. After adjusting for other factors, the odds of a recorded death overnight were 5.99 times the daytime odds (OR 5.99, interval 3.91–9.19). The model predicts a recorded death of 26.7% for an older pedestrian at night, against 8.6% for an occupant. They compare casualties in police records, and they are not risks for the whole population.

What the crash evidence asks of design

  • Night risk points to lighting, speed at night and visibility at crossings
  • Age effect (OR 1.35 per 10 years) points to crossing time, refuges and step-free routes
  • Pedestrian odds (OR 3.89 (2.08–7.27)) point to separation and lower speed
  • The register lacks speed, lighting and location — better data would sharpen every measure

15 / 24

Each finding points to a design response. Higher risk at night points to lighting, lower speeds at night and visible crossings. The odds of a recorded death rise with age, OR 1.35 (1.20–1.51) per 10 years, which points to longer crossing times, refuges and step-free routes. Pedestrians have clearly higher odds than vehicle occupants, although the exact size depends on how one unclear victim code is classed. The register has no speed, lighting or location fields, so better data would sharpen every measure.

What pedestrians report

Pulatova (2024) intercept survey at district centres; sample size and dates not reported.

16 / 24

The chart shows what pedestrians told Pulatova (2024) in an intercept survey at district centres. 73.1% see no infrastructure for people with disabilities, 66.1% say crossings are missing and 64.2% wait a long time to cross. 47.5% say footways are used as car parking, 46.1% report no shade, and 40.9% report disrespectful drivers. The survey does not report its sample size or dates, so treat the shares as indications.

Gaps in the norms

  • Crossing spacing is not regulated; an informal 200 m principle is not enforced
  • The urban limit fell from 70 to 60 km/h — still lethal at uncontrolled crossings
  • Parked cars hide pedestrians even at 40 km/h, worst on multi-lane streets
  • Pulatova recommends refuge islands and pedestrian priority in street design

17 / 24

Pulatova found gaps in Uzbekistan's street design norms. The norms do not regulate crossing spacing, and the principle that crossings should be at least 200 m apart is applied only informally. The urban speed limit fell from 70 to 60 km/h, but that is still a lethal speed at uncontrolled crossings. Parked cars hide pedestrians from drivers even at 40 km/h. She recommends refuge islands and pedestrian priority in street design.

Crossing gaps and school routes

  • Main road here: trunk to secondary, study districts, 1,169.6 km
  • 31.1% of it lies more than 200 m from the nearest mapped crossing
  • A distance to a crossing, not the informal 200 m spacing between crossings
  • 287 of 486 schools have no signalised crossing within 300 m
  • OSM misses crossings and schools: check the candidates in the field in LAB8

18 / 24

The map marks points on main roads, here trunk to secondary roads in the study districts, that lie more than 200 m from the nearest mapped crossing. This measures the distance from the road to a crossing, not the informal 200 m spacing between crossings that Pulatova describes. Of the 1,169.6 km of main road, 31.1% lies that far from a crossing, and 287 of 486 schools have no signalised crossing within 300 m. OpenStreetMap misses some crossings and schools, so each result is a place to check in the field, and LAB8 repeats this analysis with your own audit.

Local case: the Bo'zsuv canal

  • Canals cut walking routes and concentrate crossings at a few bridges
  • TSTU study of pedestrian safety and comfort along and across the Bo'zsuv canal
  • Footways used as parking (47.5% in Pulatova's survey) push people into the carriageway
  • Canal banks can become continuous, shaded walking routes

19 / 24

Canals cut walking routes and force people to cross at a few bridges. A TSTU study looked at pedestrian safety and comfort along and across the Bo'zsuv canal. When footways are used as parking, which 47.5% of Pulatova's respondents report, walkers are pushed into the carriageway. Canal banks could instead become continuous, shaded walking routes.

Redesigning a crossing

Schematic for P8: kerb extensions and a refuge turn one long uncontrolled crossing into two short ones.

The schematic compares one crossing before and after a redesign. Before, an uncontrolled zebra crosses four lanes, and a parked car hides the pedestrian from drivers. After, kerb extensions with ramps and a refuge island split the crossing into two short stages, and parking is set back from the crossing. Signals or a raised table can be added, and P8 asks you to redesign a crossing in the same way.

Key takeaways

  • Size footways for comfort at the narrowest point
  • Speed decides survival; crossings decide exposure
  • Design for the slowest and most vulnerable walker
  • Tashkent's evidence points to night, age, crossings and footway parking

22 / 24

Size footways for comfort at their narrowest point. Speed decides whether people survive a crash, and crossings decide how long they are exposed. Design for the slowest and most vulnerable walker, not the average. In Tashkent, the evidence points to night, age, crossings and parking on footways.
