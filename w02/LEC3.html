Week 2 · Lecture 1

Transport data and data quality

LEC3 · Urban Transport Systems Planning and Design · TSTU · Prof. J. Sodikov

1 / 27

What you will be able to do after today

  • Choose methods to observe vehicle, passenger, pedestrian and cycle flows
  • Explain how origin–destination data are collected and inferred
  • Plan sampling and observation periods, allowing for seasonality
  • Judge how missing or wrong data change a decision

2 / 27

This lecture is about where the numbers in a transport study come from. You will learn how to observe flows of vehicles, passengers, pedestrians and cyclists, and how origin–destination data are collected or inferred. You will also plan when and how often to observe, allowing for the seasons. Finally, you will judge how missing or wrong data can change a decision.

A data programme starts from the decision

  • Which indicator, for which decision, at what precision?
  • Which periods: peak hour, whole day, weekday, season?
  • Which technique: manual, automatic, survey, passive data?
  • What it costs, and who keeps the data afterwards
  • Design the whole data programme before collecting anything

3 / 27

Collecting data costs time and money, so start from the decision that the data must support. First name the indicator, the decision it serves and how precise it needs to be. Then choose the periods to observe and the technique, from manual counts to passive data. Design the whole data programme, including its cost and who keeps the data, before you collect anything.

Data collection techniques

TechniqueWhat it givesMain limitation
Manual classified countVehicles by class, occupancy samplesShort periods; observer error
Point check at a stopBus load, headway, boardingsOne location only
Ride check on boardBoardings and alightings by stopLabour-intensive; small sample
Automatic counters, fare and location systemsContinuous counts, travel times, tap-insNot open in Tashkent; need validation
GPS or video surveySpeeds, paths, conditionsProcessing effort; privacy
Interview surveyTrip purpose, origin, destination, attitudesSampling bias; recall

4 / 27

Each technique gives different information and has its own weakness. Manual counts, checks at a stop and checks on board cover short periods, one place or a small sample, and ride checks need a lot of labour. Automatic counters and fare or location systems give continuous data, but in Tashkent they are not open and their data need validation. Interview surveys tell you trip purpose, origin and destination, but the sample can be biased and people may not remember their trips well.

Traffic variables (Knoop)

  • Flow q: vehicles passing a point per hour
  • Density k: vehicles per kilometre at an instant
  • Speed: time-mean (at a point) differs from space-mean (over a road section)
  • q = k × space-mean speed links the three
  • Local measurements (a counter) and instantaneous ones (an aerial photo) see different things

5 / 27

Flow (q) is the number of vehicles passing a point per hour. Density (k) is the number of vehicles on one kilometre of road at one moment. Speed can be averaged at a point (time-mean speed) or over a road section (space-mean speed), and the two are not the same. Flow equals density times space-mean speed, which links the three variables. So a counter at one point and an aerial photo of a whole section measure different things.

Counting people, not only vehicles

  • Pedestrian and cycle counts at screenlines and crossings
  • Occupancy samples by vehicle class turn vehicle flows into person flows
  • Boardings and alightings at stops show who uses the corridor by bus
  • Observe from safe positions; record no personal data (syllabus §6)

6 / 27

Vehicle counts alone do not show how many people use a street. You can count pedestrians and cyclists at chosen lines across the street and at crossings, and sample how many people ride in each class of vehicle (occupancy). Occupancy turns vehicle flows into person flows, and boardings and alightings at stops show who uses the corridor by bus. Stand where you are safe when you observe, and record no personal data, as the syllabus requires.

Origin–destination data

  • Classic sources: household surveys, roadside interviews, plate matching
  • Smart-card tap-ins give origins; the next tap-in suggests the destination
  • Trip chaining and transfer inference rest on stated assumptions
  • Validate inferred matrices against counts before using them
  • Tashkent: no open OD matrix, GTFS feed or link counts were found

7 / 27

An origin–destination (OD) matrix shows how many trips go from each place to each other place. Classic sources are household surveys, roadside interviews and matching number plates seen at two points. With smart cards, a tap-in gives the origin, and the next tap-in suggests where the previous trip ended. This inference rests on stated assumptions, so check the matrix against counts before you use it. The course found no open OD matrix, timetable feed (GTFS) or link counts for Tashkent, so you must collect such data yourself.

Sampling

  • A count is a sample of days, hours and locations — state which
  • Random sampling supports estimates of an average; purposive sampling does not
  • Repeat counts on several days to see day-to-day variation
  • Normukhammadov's survey touched 57% of segments, 29% of length — and avoided roads without facilities
  • Coverage itself is a finding: what was not observed, and why?

8 / 27

A count covers only some days, hours and places, so it is a sample, and you should say which. A random sample lets you estimate an average; a purposive sample, chosen on purpose, does not. Counting on several days shows how much the numbers change from day to day. Normukhammadov's GPS survey touched 57% of segments but only 29% of their length, and it avoided roads without cycle facilities. Gaps like this are findings in themselves: report what was not observed and why.

Observation periods and seasonality

  • Peak hour, inter-peak, evening and night carry different users
  • Weekday versus weekend; school term versus holidays
  • Tashkent heat: 15 days at or above 40 °C in 2025, peak 43.9 °C
  • Mean daily maximum 38.7 °C in July, 13.9 °C in November — walking and cycling change with it
  • A survey spanning July to November mixes seasons; report it

9 / 27

Different people travel at different times: the peak hour, the inter-peak, the evening and the night carry different users. Weekdays differ from weekends, and school terms differ from holidays. In Tashkent in 2025 there were 15 days at or above 40 °C, with a peak of 43.9 °C. The mean daily maximum was 38.7 °C in July but 13.9 °C in November, and walking and cycling change with the weather. So a survey that runs from July to November mixes seasons, and its report should say so.

Missing data and decisions

  • Missing completely at random: less precision, no bias
  • Missing at random given what you know: correctable with care
  • Informative missingness: the gap is related to the outcome — bias
  • Gaps in time (a missing month) break seasonal and before–after comparisons
  • Always report how much is missing and how you handled it

11 / 27

Data can be missing in three ways, and each has a different effect. If values are missing completely at random, you lose precision but your results are not biased. If they are missing at random given what you already know, careful methods can correct for the gap. If the gap is related to the outcome itself (informative missingness), the results are biased. Gaps in time, such as a missing month, also break seasonal and before–after comparisons, so always report how much is missing and how you dealt with it.

Tashkent case 1: auditing the police crash register

  • Sodikov et al. (TIP, under review) — 2016 Tashkent register

12 / 27

The first Tashkent case is an audit of the police crash register for 2016. The study is by Sodikov and colleagues and is under review in the journal Traffic Injury Prevention. The raw register cannot be shared, so the course works only with the counts reported in the manuscript's tables.

How raw data shrink to an analysis frame

Redrawn from Figure 1 of Sodikov et al. (under review). Every exclusion is counted and explained.

13 / 27

The chart shows, step by step, how the raw register becomes the data set used for analysis (the analysis frame). It starts with 4,511 non-blank crash records; removing 136 duplicate rows leaves the crash records, which list 5,277 casualties. Further exclusions, such as the August outage, lead to a primary frame of 3,625 casualties in single-victim crashes. Every exclusion is counted and explained, so a reader can check it, and you should report your own cleaning in the same way.

What the audit found

  • 136 duplicate rows, some re-entered verbatim
  • August held only 29 crash records — a four-week outage, excluded
  • District recorded only January–April; crash type changed from text to unexplained codes in September
  • Casualty counts agree with age fields for 96.9% of records
  • No road-user role per casualty, no 30-day death window

14 / 27

The audit found 136 duplicate rows, some of them typed in again word for word. August held only 29 crash records, which points to a four-week break in recording, so the month was excluded. The district was recorded only from January to April, and from September the crash type changed from text to codes that nobody explained. Casualty counts matched the age fields in 96.9% of records. However, the register gives no road-user role for each casualty and no 30-day window for counting a road death.

Informative missingness: missing age

Recorded death is 19.6% vs 2.5% when age is missing vs recorded: dropping incomplete rows would bias results.

15 / 27

Here the missing values are not random. Among casualties whose age is missing, 19.6% have a recorded death, against 2.5% when the age is recorded. So whether age is missing is linked to the outcome: this is informative missingness. If you simply dropped the incomplete rows, you would remove a group with many deaths and bias the results.

Handling choices move the answer

  • 176 (4.9%) casualties have no road-user type
  • Pedestrian odds ratio: OR 2.60–3.89 depending on how unmapped codes are read
  • OR 4.44 (complete cases) vs 3.89 (multiple imputation)
  • Only 3,393 (93.6%) casualties are complete on all three predictors
  • Report the range, not one convenient number

16 / 27

In the register, 176 casualties (4.9%) have no road-user type, so the analyst must decide how to treat them. The odds ratio (OR) compares the odds of a recorded death for pedestrians with the odds for vehicle occupants. Depending on how the unmapped codes are read, it ranges from 2.60 to 3.89. Using only the 3,393 (93.6%) casualties with no missing road-user type, age or time gives 4.44, against 3.89 with multiple imputation. The direction of the result stays the same while its size moves, so report the range and log every choice.

Tashkent case 2: cleaning a GPS field survey

  • Normukhammadov (2026), panoramic camera and GPS rides, July–November 2025

17 / 27

The second case comes from Normukhammadov's (2026) thesis on cycling in Tashkent. From July to November 2025 he recorded rides through the city with a panoramic camera and GPS. In LAB2 you will work with a small real extract of these raw data, so you will meet the same problems yourself.

Five real problems in one survey

  • Whole clips copied into several folders — counted twice
  • Cross-campaign contamination: 20 clips in both campaigns; 30–31% of Campaign II inertial rows removed
  • Clip timers restarting at zero: sorting by them inflated distance ≈500×
  • 253 rows, up to 112 m/s — impossible on a bicycle
  • Folder names that gave the wrong route

18 / 27

Even a careful survey contained five kinds of error. Whole clips were copied into several folders and so counted twice. Also, 20 clips appeared in both campaigns, and fixing this removed 30–31% of Campaign II accelerometer and gyroscope rows. Clip timers restarted at zero, so sorting by them inflated the distance about 500 times, and 253 rows showed speeds up to 112 m/s, impossible on a bicycle. After cleaning, 239 clips remained, with 1,567,607 GPS points and 55.1 h of recording.

The same twelve minutes, sorted two ways

LAB2 sample: 1.99 km clean; 36.93 km in file order; 59.0 km sorted by clip timer. 6,273 raw rows, 3,123 duplicate rows.

19 / 27

The figure shows the same twelve minutes of real riding from the LAB2 sample, processed in three ways. Cleaned and sorted by time, the track is 1.99 km long and stays within a small area. Left in file order it measures 36.93 km, and sorted by the clip timer it measures 59.0 km. The raw file has 6,273 rows, including 3,123 duplicate rows, so a sorting or duplication error can multiply a distance many times. In LAB2 you will compute these three lengths yourself.

Validation gates

  • After each cleaning step, test a quantity you can predict independently
  • Median step distance must match speed × time step: 0.25 m vs 0.26 m
  • A discrepancy of two orders of magnitude signals a sorting or duplication error
  • Write the gate down before you run the step

20 / 27

A validation gate is a check you run after each cleaning step, using a quantity you can predict in another way. For GPS data, the median distance between points should equal speed multiplied by the time step; in the thesis these were 0.25 m and 0.26 m. If the two differ by a factor of about a hundred (two orders of magnitude), the data were probably sorted wrongly or duplicated. Write each gate down before you run the step, not after you see the result.

Open data can be badly wrong: population

Kontur (2023) gives Olmazor 74,918 residents; Pulatova quotes 387.7 thousand (2022). The model places the city's people in the south.

21 / 27

Open datasets can be wrong by a large margin. Kontur's 2023 population model gives Olmazor district 74,918 residents, while Pulatova quotes 387.7 thousand for 2022. The model places much of the city's population in the south, in the wrong districts. Pulatova's figures come from different years, but that cannot explain a gap of this size. Before you use any open dataset, compare it with an independent total, as you did in LAB1.

Not recorded is not absent: OSM sidewalk tags

Ergashova (2026): most streets carry no sidewalk information in OSM (grey); figure reproduced with permission (Uzbek legend).

22 / 27

This map from Ergashova (2026) shows what OpenStreetMap (OSM) records about sidewalks on each street. Most streets are grey, which means OSM holds no sidewalk information for them. That does not mean those streets have no sidewalks; it only means nobody has recorded them. Treat a blank in open data as unknown, not as zero, and check on the ground. Discussion

Which of your P1 indicators would be most damaged by missing data — and how would you notice?
  • Name the indicator, the likely gap and the check you would run.

23 / 27

This question asks you to apply the lecture to your own P1 indicators. Pick the indicator that missing data would harm most, and name the gap you expect. Then describe the check that would reveal the gap, such as comparing with an independent total or looking at coverage by time and place. Your answer will help you plan the P2 survey and its data-quality log.

Key takeaways

  • Design the data programme from the decision and the indicator
  • State sample, periods and season; coverage gaps are findings
  • Missing data can be informative; report how much and how handled
  • Audit and validate before analysing — local surveys and open data alike

24 / 27

Design data collection from the decision and the indicator it serves, not from what is easy to count. State your sample, observation periods and season, and treat gaps in coverage as findings. Missing data can be informative, so report how much is missing and how you handled it. Audit and validate every dataset before analysis, whether it is a local survey or open data.
