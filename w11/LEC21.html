Parking and kerbside management

LEC21 · Urban Transport Systems Planning and Design · TSTU · Prof. J. Sodikov

What you will be able to do after today

  • Explain why parking supply and price shape how people travel
  • Measure occupancy, duration and turnover from a kerbside survey
  • Compare pricing, time limits and enforcement as demand-management tools
  • Allocate the kerb between buses, deliveries, taxis, parking and pedestrians

2 / 23

This lecture treats parking as a tool of transport policy. You will explain why the supply and price of parking shape how people travel. You will measure occupancy, duration and turnover from a kerbside survey, and compare pricing, time limits and enforcement. Finally, you will share the kerb between buses, deliveries, taxis, parking and pedestrians.

Parking is transport policy

  • Every car trip begins and ends in a parking space
  • Free, plentiful parking at the destination is one of the strongest reasons to drive
  • Minimum parking requirements turn land into car storage and raise building costs
  • Parking decisions are made by many owners — the city controls mainly the kerb
  • Parking links land use, traffic, the local economy and equity

3 / 23

Every car trip starts and ends in a parking space. A published course on urban transportation planning presents free, plentiful parking at the destination as one of the strongest reasons to drive. Minimum parking requirements turn land into car storage and make buildings more expensive. Many owners decide about parking, but the city controls mainly the kerb, where parking meets land use, traffic, the local economy and equity.

European cities changed direction

  • From accommodation (build more spaces) to regulation (manage the spaces you have)
  • Instruments: on-street pricing, time limits, residents' permits, caps on new supply
  • Revenue reinvested in public transport, walking and street improvements
  • Enforcement and public communication decided success

4 / 23

Many European cities moved from building more parking spaces to managing the spaces they already have. ITDP (2011) calls this a change from accommodation to regulation. Their tools include on-street pricing, time limits, residents' permits and caps on new supply. The revenue went into public transport, walking and better streets, and enforcement and clear public communication decided whether the policies worked.

The four measures of a parking survey

  • Accumulation: vehicles parked at a moment
  • Occupancy: accumulation ÷ legal spaces — peak and average
  • Duration: how long each vehicle stays
  • Turnover: vehicles per space per day
  • Plus illegal parking: on footways, double-parked, in bus stops

5 / 23

A parking survey gives four main measures. Accumulation is the number of vehicles parked at one moment, and occupancy divides it by the legal spaces, at the peak and on average. Duration is how long each vehicle stays, and turnover is how many vehicles use each space in a day. A good survey also records illegal parking on footways, in double rows and in bus stops. LAB11 produces all of these.

Worked example: duration and turnover

  • A block of 10 spaces is patrolled every 30 minutes from 08:00 to 20:00
  • A car seen on 4 consecutive rounds stayed about 4 × 0.5 = 2 h
  • 60 different vehicles in the day → turnover = 60 ÷ 10 = 6 vehicles per space
  • If 2 commuters stay 10 h each, they use 20 of the 120 space-hours
  • Stays shorter than the patrol interval can be missed entirely

6 / 23

In this example, a block of 10 spaces is checked every 30 minutes from 08:00 to 20:00. A car seen on 4 rounds in a row stayed about 4 × 0.5 = 2 h. If 60 different vehicles park in the day, turnover is 60 ÷ 10 = 6 vehicles per space. Two commuters who each stay 10 h use 20 of the 120 space-hours. A visit shorter than the patrol interval can be missed completely.

How to run a kerbside survey

  • Inventory first: legal spaces, no-stopping zones, bus stops, loading bays
  • Patrol (beat) survey: record vehicle code, class and position each round
  • Replace plates with codes in the field — no personal data (syllabus §6)
  • Separate continuous log for deliveries and taxis, which stay briefly
  • Survey on a normal weekday and on a market day; record the weather

7 / 23

Start with an inventory of legal spaces, no-stopping zones, bus stops and loading bays. In a patrol, or beat, survey you walk the same route each round and record each vehicle's code, class and position. Replace number plates with codes in the field, so that no personal data are kept. Log deliveries and taxis separately and continuously, because they stay only briefly, and survey on a normal weekday and a market day.

Managing parking demand

  • Price: makes long stays expensive where space is scarce
  • Time limits: cheap to introduce, need constant enforcement
  • Permits: protect residents, can lock in free storage
  • Target: a few free spaces on every block, so nobody circles
  • Supply caps and removal of minimum requirements in new development

8 / 23

Each tool for managing parking demand has strengths and limits. Pricing makes long stays expensive where space is scarce, while time limits are cheap to introduce but need constant enforcement. Permits protect residents but can turn the kerb into free car storage. The aim is a few free spaces on every block, so that nobody has to circle. In new development, cities can cap supply and remove minimum parking requirements.

The kerb is the scarcest space in the street

  • Bus stops: passengers boarding and alighting
  • Deliveries: short, frequent, often in the morning
  • Taxis and ride-hailing: very short pick-ups
  • Parking: long occupation of the same metres
  • Pedestrians and cyclists: crossings need clear sight lines at the kerb

9 / 23

Many users want the same metres of kerb. Bus passengers board and alight there, deliveries need short and frequent stops, often in the morning, and taxis need very short pick-ups. Parked cars hold the same metres for a long time. Pedestrians and cyclists need clear sight lines at the kerb where they cross.

Sharing the kerb by time of day

Illustrative plan: the same flex zone serves loading in the morning and short-stay parking later.

10 / 23

The figure is an illustrative kerb plan for a market street, with metres along the kerb across and hours of the day up the side. The bus stop and the taxi stop keep their space all day. The flex zone serves loading only in the morning, then paid short-stay parking, and is free for residents at night. You will adapt this layout for your own corridor in P11.

Tashkent: what we know about parking

  • OpenStreetMap inventory, the students' surveys, and a synthetic kerbside survey

11 / 23

The second part of the lecture looks at parking in Tashkent. It uses the OpenStreetMap inventory, surveys by TSTU students and a synthetic kerbside survey for the lab.

Mapped off-street parking

2,021 OSM car parks, of which 1,959 polygons covering 339 ha (October 2026 extract).

12 / 23

The map shows the off-street car parks mapped in OpenStreetMap, in red, over a district shading of estimated spaces per km². The October 2026 extract holds 2,021 car parks, and 1,959 of them are polygons covering 339 ha. The shading uses a capacity proxy, which the lecture explains next. Chilonzor has the highest estimated density, mainly from car parks in its microdistrict courtyards.

From area to spaces: a capacity proxy

  • Only 25 car parks carry a capacity tag
  • Their median is 22.9 m² per space, including aisles
  • Applied to all polygons: about 148,000 spaces
  • That is 48.7 per 1,000 residents, against 197 cars / 1,000 in 2023
  • Kerbside parking is mostly unmapped — the real supply is larger

13 / 23

Only 25 mapped car parks carry a capacity tag, and their median is 22.9 m² per space, including aisles. Dividing all car-park area by this value gives about 148,000 spaces. That is 48.7 per 1,000 residents, while Tashkent had 197 cars per 1,000 residents in 2023. This is an estimate, not a count, and most kerbside parking is not mapped, so the real supply is larger.

What the open data does not say

Type, fee and capacity are missing for most car parks; 110 are tagged as street-side areas.

14 / 23

The figure shows what OpenStreetMap records about Tashkent's car parks. For most of them, the type, the fee and the capacity are not tagged. Only 110 are tagged as street-side areas, where kerbside parking is mapped as a polygon. Because the open data are so incomplete, a field survey is needed.

Parking on footways

  • 47.5% of surveyed pedestrians say footways are used as car parks
  • 40.5% say footways are not protected from the carriageway
  • 27% say vehicles driving on footways obstruct walking
  • Cars parked in the kerb lane hide pedestrians even at 40 km/h

15 / 23

In Pulatova's survey, 47.5% of pedestrians say footways are used as car parks, and 40.5% say footways are not protected from the carriageway. Another 27% say vehicles driving on footways get in the way of walking. Cars parked in the kerb lane also hide pedestrians, so that even at 40 km/h a driver cannot see them. The thesis does not report the sample size.

The same footway, three claims on it

  • Cyclists already ride on sidewalks for 66.4% of their riding exposure
  • 94% of the cycle-network gap closure would run along existing sidewalks
  • Pedestrians, parked cars and cyclists compete for one strip of space
  • A parking policy is also a walking and cycling policy

16 / 23

Normukhammadov's thesis found that 66.4% of the recorded riding time was on sidewalks. It also found that 94% of the links needed to close the cycle-network gaps would run along existing sidewalks. So pedestrians, parked cars and cyclists compete for one strip of space. A parking policy is therefore also a walking and cycling policy, as Week 8 showed.

LAB11 site: Avliyoota St beside Mirobod bozori

Real OSM location; four kerb segments with 53 legal spaces. The survey data are SYNTHETIC.

17 / 23

The LAB11 site is on Avliyoota St beside Mirobod bozori, a real location taken from OpenStreetMap. Four kerb segments hold 53 legal spaces: A1 and A2 on the market side, and B1 and B2 opposite. The bus stop takes part of segment A1. The survey data in the lab are synthetic.

A day on the kerb

SYNTHETIC: peak kerb occupancy 89% (segment A1: 100%); at the peak, demand is 1.08 times the legal spaces.

18 / 23

The chart follows parked vehicles on the four segments through a synthetic survey day. The line shows vehicles at the legal kerb. The shaded area above it shows vehicles parked off the kerb: on the footway, in a second row or in the bus stop. At the peak the kerb is 89% full, and segment A1 is 100% full. Peak demand is 1.08 times the legal spaces, so some vehicles park off the legal kerb.

Few long stays, most of the space

SYNTHETIC: long stays are 10% of vehicles but 51% of vehicle-hours; mean stay 1.65 h.

19 / 23

The left panel shows that most vehicles stay only briefly. The right panel shows that long stays of more than 3 h are 10% of vehicles but fill 51% of the vehicle-hours. The mean stay is 1.65 h. So a parking policy should target the long stays, not the short visits of shoppers. These are synthetic results.

This question asks you to name who parks on your corridor's kerb in the middle of the morning. Then decide who would lose most if the city started charging for that space. Key takeaways

  • Parking supply and price are among the strongest levers on car use
  • Measure occupancy, duration and turnover before proposing a policy
  • Long stays occupy most space-hours — target them
  • Footway parking in Tashkent is a walking, cycling and safety problem

21 / 23

The supply and price of parking are among the strongest levers on car use. Measure occupancy, duration and turnover before you propose a policy. Long stays take up most of the space-hours, so target them. In Tashkent, parking on footways is a problem for walking, cycling and safety.
