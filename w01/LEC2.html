What you will be able to do after today

  • Distinguish goals, objectives, targets and indicators
  • Explain the five policy objectives and where they conflict
  • Read a city's transport targets critically
  • Define measurable outcome indicators for a Tashkent corridor

2 / 25

The first lecture showed how to define a transport problem; this lecture shows how to turn that problem into objectives. You will learn the difference between goals, objectives, targets and indicators, and why policy objectives often conflict. You will also read Tashkent's own transport targets critically and define measurable outcome indicators for a corridor.

From problems to objectives

  • Vision: the kind of city we want (qualitative)
  • Objective: a direction of change (more, less, safer, fairer)
  • Target: a value and a date for an indicator
  • Indicator: what is measured, how, by whom, how often
  • Measures (projects, policies) come after, and are judged against the objectives

3 / 25

Planning moves from a broad vision to things you can measure. A vision describes the kind of city we want, in words, and an objective gives a direction of change, such as more, less, safer or fairer. A target adds a value and a date for an indicator, and the indicator says what is measured, how, by whom and how often. Measures, meaning projects and policies, come only after this chain and are judged against the objectives. In the SUMP Guidelines this belongs to Phase 2, strategy development.

The SUMP cycle: where objectives sit

SUMP Guidelines (2019): 4 phases, 12 steps. Weeks 1–3 use Phases 1–2.

4 / 25

The SUMP Guidelines (2019) organise a sustainable urban mobility plan as a cycle of 4 phases, 12 steps. Phase 1 is preparation and analysis, and Phase 2 is strategy development, where the vision, objectives, targets and indicators are set. Phases 3 and 4 cover planning the measures, and then implementation and monitoring. Weeks 1 to 3 of this course use Phases 1 and 2.

Five objectives of contemporary transport policy

  • Mobility: ease and speed of movement
  • Accessibility: reaching jobs, schools, services and each other
  • Safety: fewer deaths and serious injuries, especially of vulnerable users
  • Efficiency: resources (space, money, energy) per person served
  • Environmental sustainability: emissions, air quality, noise, land
  • Cutting across all five: who gains and who pays (equity)

5 / 25

Contemporary transport policy usually pursues five objectives. Mobility is the ease and speed of movement, and accessibility is reaching jobs, schools, services and each other. Safety means fewer deaths and serious injuries, especially among vulnerable users. Efficiency is about the space, money and energy used per person served, and environmental sustainability covers emissions, air quality, noise and land. Equity, meaning who gains and who pays, cuts across all five and is developed further in Week 3.

This table gives one example indicator for each objective and shows where Tashkent data for it can come from. Mobility uses door-to-door travel time from field runs or GPS tracks, and accessibility uses residential land within a 15-minute walk of a metro station (LAB3). An indicator is only useful if you have a method and the data to measure it, and the later labs and practicals teach these methods.

Three E's: environment, economy, equity

  • Urban transport decisions trade environment, economy and equity against each other
  • No technical method removes the trade-off; it makes it visible
  • Planning is also politics: who sets the objectives decides the answer
  • Week 12 returns to institutions and participation

7 / 25

A published course on urban transportation planning frames urban transport decisions as a trade-off between environment, economy and equity, the 'three E's'. No technical method can remove this trade-off, but good analysis makes it visible. Planning is also political, because whoever sets the objectives largely decides the answer. Week 12 returns to institutions and public participation.

Goals conflict by design (Walker)

  • Public transport is asked to maximise ridership and to cover everyone
  • Ridership favours frequent service where demand is dense
  • Coverage spreads service thinly so that no area is left out
  • The same budget cannot do both — so decide the split explicitly

8 / 25

Walker (2008) points out that public transport is often asked to do two things at once: maximise ridership and cover everyone. Ridership goals favour frequent service where demand is dense, while coverage goals spread service thinly so that no area is left out. With the same budget you cannot fully do both, so the split between them should be decided openly. Week 6 applies this idea to service design.

Where objectives collide

  • Speed versus safety: faster arterials raise the stakes at every crossing
  • Vehicle capacity versus bus priority: one lane, two uses
  • Cycling versus walking: gap closure puts 94% of new cycle links on sidewalks
  • Coverage versus frequency in public transport
  • Short-term traffic relief versus long-term induced demand

9 / 25

Objectives often pull in different directions on the same street; for example, one lane cannot serve both general traffic and bus priority. Faster arterials, the main roads that carry city-wide movement, raise the danger at every crossing. In the gap-closure plan of Normukhammadov (2026), 94% of the new cycle links by length would run along existing sidewalks, which helps cycling but takes space from walking. Public transport faces coverage versus frequency, and short-term traffic relief can lead to more traffic later through induced demand. For each trade-off someone has to decide which objective wins, and that choice should be made explicit.

Tashkent context in numbers

  • 3.04 million residents at the start of 2024
  • 197 cars / 1,000 residents in 2023, the highest in the country
  • Private vehicles in Uzbekistan growing by ≈7.5% a year
  • 79% of trips by private car, by a 2022 presidential briefing
  • Annual PM2.5 of 31.5 µg/m³ in 2024, more than six times the WHO guideline

11 / 25

Tashkent had 3.04 million residents at the start of 2024, and in 2023 it had 197 cars per 1,000 residents, the highest rate in the country. Private vehicles in Uzbekistan are growing by ≈7.5% a year, and annual PM2.5 in Tashkent was 31.5 µg/m³ in 2024, more than six times the WHO guideline. A 2022 presidential briefing put the share of trips by private car at 79%. This number was not measured in a travel survey, so treat it with care. All of these figures come from chapter 1 of Normukhammadov (2026), which cites national statistics and other published sources.

12 / 25

This table lists the 2025 targets of the Tashkent transport concept for 2022–2025, from the Ministry of Transport as quoted by Pulatova (2024). Most targets describe the system itself: bus operating speed +17%, service regularity 95%, route headways −35% and junction vehicle capacity +15%. A headway is the time between one bus and the next on a route, and regularity is how evenly the gaps between buses stay the same. These are outputs; the public transport share of 43% (2.7 million passengers a day) and −20% cars in the city centre are outcomes. For any target, check whether it measures the system or what happens to people.

Reading the targets critically

  • No target for walking, cycling or road deaths (Pulatova notes the missing pedestrian part)
  • More junction capacity for cars can pull against fewer cars in the centre
  • The public transport share target needs a measured baseline — the 79% car share is a briefing figure
  • Most targets are outputs; few say who benefits

13 / 25

The concept has no target for walking, cycling or road deaths, and Pulatova (2024) notes that the pedestrian part is missing. Some targets also pull against each other: more junction capacity for cars can work against having fewer cars in the city centre. The public transport share target needs a measured starting value, and the 79% car share figure comes from a briefing, not a survey. Most targets are outputs and few say who benefits. A stronger target would describe an effect on people and state its starting value, how it is measured and who is responsible for it.

Kilometres announced or outcomes delivered?

  • In 2020 the city announced 656 km of pedestrian and bicycle lanes
  • A length target rewards building anywhere; a connectivity target rewards building where it joins
  • By length 23.4% of the audited network is cycle path, by count 32.6% of segments
  • The same infrastructure looks different depending on the indicator chosen

14 / 25

In 2020 the city announced 656 km of pedestrian and bicycle lanes. A target based on length rewards building anywhere, while a target based on connectivity rewards building where new paths join the network. The choice of indicator also changes the picture: in Normukhammadov's audit, cycle paths are 23.4% of the network by length but 32.6% of the segments by count. Indicators are not neutral, because they steer what gets built.

What makes a good indicator?

  • Relevant: it moves when the objective is met, not just when money is spent
  • Measurable with data you can actually collect, at a stated frequency
  • Has a baseline, a target value and a target year
  • Can be split by user group, area or time of day (equity)
  • Has an owner: the organisation that measures and reports it

15 / 25

A good indicator moves when the objective is achieved, not just when money is spent. It must be measurable with data you can actually collect, at a stated frequency. It needs a baseline, a target value and a target year. It should be possible to split it by user group, area or time of day, so that you can check equity. It also needs an owner, the organisation that measures and reports it.

Inputs, outputs, outcomes

LevelExample on a bus corridorTells you
InputBudget spent, kilometres of bus lane builtEffort
OutputBus speed, headway, regularitySystem performance
OutcomePassengers carried, travel time door to door, deathsEffect on people
ImpactMode share, air quality, access to jobsLong-term change

16 / 25

This table shows that indicators sit at four levels, from what is spent to what changes for people. On a bus corridor, kilometres of bus lane built is an input and bus speed is an output, but neither tells you whether people are better off. Outcomes, such as passengers carried and door-to-door travel time, show the effect on people, and impacts, such as mode share, show long-term change. Tashkent's concept mostly sets output targets, so make sure your own 5–7 indicators in P1 include outcomes.

Indicators from Tashkent research you can reuse

  • Pulatova: walk area within 1,200 m (15-min walk) on the network ÷ circle area
  • Ergashova: metro stations reachable on foot from each mahalla; footway density
  • Normukhammadov: fragments, largest connected length, origin–destination pairs reachable
  • Sodikov et al.: recorded death among police-recorded casualties
  • Each comes with a published method — cite it and reuse it

17 / 25

Several Tashkent studies already offer indicators with a published method that you can cite and reuse. Pulatova measures the area you can reach in a 1,200 m walk along the street network, which takes about 15 minutes. She divides it by the area of a circle with a 1,200 m radius, so the index shows how much of that circle you can really reach on foot. Ergashova counts the metro stations reachable on foot from each mahalla, Normukhammadov measures how fragmented the cycle network is, and Sodikov and colleagues use recorded deaths among police-recorded casualties. You will recompute Pulatova's index yourself in LAB3.

An indicator in use: Pulatova's walk index

No district centre reaches 'good' (80–89 = good). Values from Pulatova (2024), Appendix 9.

18 / 25

This chart shows Pulatova's walk index for the centres of Tashkent's districts, using the values in Appendix 9 of her dissertation. The index compares the area reachable by a network walk with the area of the full circle. A higher value therefore means more of the circle can really be reached on foot. Scores of 80–89 count as good, and no district centre reaches that level: values range from 78 at Amir Temur Square down to 26 in Yashnobod. In Week 3 you will read this appendix critically, because two of its district figures contradict each other.

Worked example: indicators for an Abdulla Qodiriy corridor

  • Problem: people on foot and bicycle cannot cross or ride safely along the street
  • Objective 1: safer crossing — indicator: crossings per km with a signal or refuge
  • Objective 2: access to the metro — indicator: residents within a 15-minute walk
  • Objective 3: protected cycling — indicator: share of corridor length with a cycle path
  • Objective 4: fair benefits — indicator: each of the above for older people and children

19 / 25

This worked example turns a corridor problem into objectives and indicators, using Abdulla Qodiriy street, the example corridor mapped in LAB1. The problem is that people on foot and on bicycles cannot cross or ride safely along the street. Each objective gets one indicator: safer crossing is measured by crossings per km with a signal or refuge, and metro access by residents within a 15-minute walk. Protected cycling is measured by the share of corridor length with a cycle path, and fair benefits by splitting each indicator for older people and children. It is only an illustration; in P1 you will produce your own version for your corridor.

Common mistakes

  • Only vehicle indicators (flow, speed) for a corridor used mostly by people on foot
  • Indicators with no data source or no baseline
  • Twenty indicators instead of five to seven that matter
  • Counting the same benefit twice under two names
  • No breakdown by who benefits

20 / 25

These are common mistakes when choosing indicators. One is using only vehicle indicators, such as flow and speed, on a corridor used mostly by people on foot. Others are indicators with no data source or no baseline, and twenty indicators instead of the five to seven that matter. Two more are counting the same benefit twice under two names, and giving no breakdown by who benefits, which hides equity problems. Check your Stage 1 indicators against this list.

The transport concept asks for +15% junction capacity and a 43% public transport share at the same time. Think about whether one corridor can deliver both, since more space for cars at junctions may conflict with giving road space to buses. Argue from your own corridor, decide which target you would drop, and name the indicator you would use to check the result.

Key takeaways

  • Objectives first, measures second, and judge measures against objectives
  • Policy objectives conflict; make the trade-off explicit
  • Prefer outcome indicators with a baseline, a target, a method and an owner
  • Tashkent's targets are mostly outputs and leave walking and safety out

22 / 25

Set objectives first and choose measures second, and then judge every measure against the objectives. Policy objectives conflict, so make each trade-off explicit instead of hiding it. Prefer outcome indicators that have a baseline, a target, a method and an owner. Tashkent's current targets are mostly outputs and leave out walking and safety.
