A systems approach to urban transport

LEC1 · Urban Transport Systems Planning and Design · TSTU · Prof. J. Sodikov

1 / 26

What you will be able to do after today

  • Describe an urban transport system as networks, services, infrastructure and operations
  • Tell the movement of vehicles apart from people's ability to reach destinations
  • Define a transport problem, its system boundaries and the users it affects
  • Start the course project on your own Tashkent corridor

2 / 26

This lecture is about looking at a city's transport as one system. By the end you should be able to describe that system and tell moving vehicles apart from helping people reach places. You should also be able to define a transport problem, its boundary and its users. The main message of the course starts here: decide which problem to solve before you choose a solution. You will also start the course project on your own Tashkent corridor.

How this course is built

  • Technical backbone: methods for designing transport systems
  • Policy backbone: urban transport planning and policy (justice, governance, decarbonisation)
  • One city as the case: Tashkent, with research by TSTU students and staff
  • One corridor per student or team, carried through all fifteen weeks
  • Tools: QGIS and spreadsheets; every number traceable to a source

3 / 26

Two main sources shape the course. One supplies the technical methods for designing transport systems; the other supplies the policy questions, such as justice, governance and decarbonisation. Tashkent is the case city throughout, with research by TSTU students and staff. You keep one corridor for the whole semester and build the course project on it in five stages. The course is TSTU's own adaptation of openly published material, which is listed in the references.

Four design perspectives, one policy lens

The technical methods look at the same system as a network, a set of services, physical assets and operations; the policy lens runs through every week.

4 / 26

The technical part of the course looks at the same transport system in four ways: as a network, as a set of services, as physical infrastructure and as operations. Each view asks different design questions about the same streets and vehicles. Policy is not a separate fifth topic; it runs through every week of the course. To plan your own corridor well, you will need all four views and the policy lens together.

What makes it a system?

  • Elements: links, nodes, vehicles, services, users, operators, rules
  • Interactions: a new bus lane changes car speeds, walking routes and shop deliveries
  • Boundary: what you analyse in detail versus what you treat as given
  • Feedback: faster roads change where people live and work over years
  • Outcomes emerge from the whole, not from any single element

5 / 26

A transport system is made of many elements: links, nodes, vehicles, services, users, operators and rules. These elements interact, so a change in one place moves effects elsewhere; for example, a new bus lane changes car speeds, walking routes and shop deliveries. Over the years there is also feedback: faster roads change where people live and work, and this can create new traffic (induced demand, covered in Week 5). You must decide a boundary, that is, what you study in detail and what you treat as given. The slide's last point sums this up: outcomes come from the whole system working together, not from any single element.

The transport system and its effects (after van Wee et al.)

Redrawn and simplified from the conceptual model in van Wee et al. (2023, ch. 2).

6 / 26

This diagram simplifies the model in chapter 2 of van Wee and colleagues (2023), and it reads from left to right. On the left are three inputs: the needs of people and firms, the locations of activities (land use) and transport resistance (the time, cost, effort and risk of a trip). Together they produce a transport volume, which is the people and goods that move. Vehicle occupancy and the choice of mode then turn people into vehicles, and this gives the traffic volume on the network. The effects come last: accessibility, safety and health, and the environment.

People moved are not vehicles moved

  • Transport volume counts people and goods; traffic volume counts vehicles
  • A full bus and a single-occupant car are each one vehicle in a traffic count
  • Policies that maximise vehicle flow can reduce the number of people served
  • Week 2 practical: compare vehicle flows with person flows on your corridor

7 / 26

Transport volume counts people and goods, while traffic volume counts vehicles. In a traffic count, a full bus and a car carrying only its driver are each one vehicle. So a policy that moves the most vehicles can end up serving fewer people. In the Week 2 practical you will measure this yourself by comparing vehicle flows with person flows on your corridor.

Mobility versus accessibility

  • Mobility: how far and how fast people can move
  • Accessibility: how easily people can reach the activities they need
  • Four components (Geurs & van Wee): land use, transport, time, the individual
  • More mobility can mean less accessibility if activities spread out
  • Plan for accessibility; treat speed as one means among several

8 / 26

Mobility is about how far and how fast people can move. Accessibility is about how easily people can reach the activities they need, such as jobs, schools and services. Geurs and van Wee (2004) describe four components of accessibility: land use, transport, time and the individual. More mobility does not always mean more accessibility: if activities spread further apart, people may move more but reach fewer of the places they need. That is why this course plans for accessibility and treats speed as only one means among several; you will read Geurs and van Wee again in Week 3.

Walking to the metro: 585 mahallas

Ergashova (2026): 435 mahallas (74.4%) have no metro station within a 15-minute walk. Figure reproduced with permission (Uzbek labels).

10 / 26

Ergashova (2026) checked, for each of Tashkent's 585 mahallas, how many metro stations can be reached within a 15-minute walk. The figure has three panels: the whole city, a zoom on the centre, and the result for each mahalla (0, 1, 2, or 3 or more stations). The result is that 435 mahallas, or 74.4%, have no metro station within a 15-minute walk. So the metro is fast and carries large numbers of passengers, yet for most mahallas it is out of walking reach. The labels are in Uzbek because the figure is reproduced with permission from the original study.

Infrastructure built is not access delivered

  • Tashkent has 49 metro stations (OSM), yet 74.4% of its 585 mahallas cannot reach one within a 15-minute walk
  • 222.7 km of protected cycle paths connect only 4 of 4,095 district-scale origin–destination pairs
  • Both are systems problems: the parts exist, the connections do not
  • Indicators that count kilometres or stations miss this

11 / 26

This slide puts two Tashkent findings side by side to make one point. The city has 49 metro stations in OpenStreetMap, yet 74.4% of its 585 mahallas cannot reach one within a 15-minute walk. Normukhammadov (2026) found that 222.7 km of protected cycle paths connect only 4 of 4,095 district-scale origin–destination pairs. In both cases the parts exist but do not link up into trips people can make, so counting kilometres or stations makes the system look better than it works. When you choose indicators for your corridor, measure whether people can reach places, not only how much was built.

Defining the problem

  • Before alternatives: what is wrong, for whom, where and when?

12 / 26

This part of the lecture is about defining the problem before looking for solutions. A good definition says what is wrong, for whom, where and when. Only after that should you start to think about alternatives.

Planning is a process, not a calculation

  • A published course on urban transportation planning sets out 'The Planning Method' as a 19-step process
  • The course keeps environment, economy and equity (the 'three E's') in view together
  • SUMP Guidelines: start from problems and opportunities, not from a preferred project
  • SUMP Guidelines: involve the people affected throughout and expect to iterate
  • Our simplified cycle on the next slide is a synthesis of both, not the 19-step list

13 / 26

Planning is a sequence of steps and choices, not one calculation that produces the answer. A published course on urban transportation planning presents 'The Planning Method' as a process of 19 steps. The SUMP Guidelines (2019) add that planning should start from problems and opportunities, not from a project someone already prefers, and that the people affected should be involved throughout. The course also keeps environment, economy and equity (the 'three E's') in view together, and you should expect to go back and repeat steps. The simplified cycle on the next slide combines both sources; it is not the original list of 19 steps.

A planning cycle for this course

Synthesis of 'The Planning Method' and the SUMP Guidelines; Week 1 covers steps 1–3.

14 / 26

This cycle is the course's own synthesis of 'The Planning Method' and the SUMP Guidelines, not a copy of either. It has eight steps: problems and opportunities, vision and objectives, indicators and targets, alternatives, analysis of effects, appraisal and choice, implementation, and monitoring and evaluation. Stakeholders and the public are involved at every step, and results often send you back to an earlier step. Week 1 covers the first three steps; later weeks deal with alternatives, appraisal and monitoring.

Symptoms, causes and disguised solutions

  • Symptom: queues at a junction. Possible causes: signal timing, turning traffic, parked cars, land use
  • 'We need a flyover' is a solution presented as a problem
  • A problem statement names who is affected, where, when and how much
  • Problem tree: one core problem, its causes below and its effects above
  • Objectives come from reversing the tree: effects become goals, causes become levers

15 / 26

A queue at a junction is a symptom; its cause could be signal timing, turning traffic, parked cars or the surrounding land use. A statement such as 'we need a flyover' is a solution presented as a problem, because it skips the question of what is actually wrong. A good problem statement names who is affected, where, when and how much. A problem tree puts one core problem in the middle, its causes below and its effects above. Reversing the tree gives objectives: the effects become goals and the causes become levers you can act on, and you will use this method in P1.

Problem tree: Tashkent's cycle network

Built from the problem statement in Normukhammadov (2026, ch. 1): composition, connectivity, strategy and data problems.

16 / 26

This problem tree is built from the problem statement in chapter 1 of Normukhammadov (2026). The core problem is that Tashkent's cycle paths form separate fragments, not a connected network. Its effects, shown above, include most riding happening on sidewalks, conflicts with pedestrians and few trips being possible on protected paths. Its causes, shown below, include building street by street with no connectivity objective, open data that miss most paths, and sidewalk space that was never formally reallocated. Each box is backed by evidence in the thesis, which is the standard to aim for in your own tree.

A well-framed problem: Normukhammadov (2026)

  • Composition: 66.4% of riding took place on sidewalks
  • Connectivity: protected paths form 149 disconnected fragments
  • Strategy: which links to build first with a limited budget
  • Data: OpenStreetMap shows 8.4 km of cycle track; the field audit found 238.7 km
  • Each dimension has a measurable indicator and a research question

17 / 26

Normukhammadov (2026) frames the problem of Tashkent's cycle network in four dimensions. Composition: 66.4% of riding took place on sidewalks; connectivity: the protected paths form 149 disconnected fragments. Strategy asks which links to build first with a limited budget. Data shows a gap: OpenStreetMap records only 8.4 km of cycle track, while the field audit found 238.7 km. Because each dimension comes with a measurable indicator and a research question, use this as a model when you write your own Stage 1 problem statement.

Drawing the system boundary

  • Spatial: the corridor plus the area whose trips use it and where effects spill over
  • Temporal: peak hour, whole day, season; one assessment year for all alternatives
  • Modal: walking, cycling, public transport, car and freight together
  • Institutional: which organisations decide, pay and operate
  • Too narrow a boundary hides displaced problems, such as traffic moving to parallel streets

18 / 26

Every study needs a boundary that says what is inside the analysis. The spatial boundary covers the corridor plus the area whose trips use it and where effects spill over. The temporal boundary sets the period (peak hour, whole day or season) and one assessment year that you use for every alternative. The modal and institutional boundaries cover all modes, including freight, and the organisations that decide, pay and operate. If the boundary is too narrow, you can miss problems that simply move, for example traffic shifting to parallel streets.

Users and their needs

  • Residents, commuters, pupils, older people, people with disabilities, couriers, shopkeepers
  • Needs differ: time, cost, safety, comfort, dignity, reliability
  • Non-users are affected too: noise, air quality, severance
  • Pulatova's survey: 73.1% report no infrastructure for people with disabilities
  • 66.1% say pedestrian crossings are missing (sample size not reported)

19 / 26

A corridor serves many different users, such as residents, commuters, pupils, older people, people with disabilities, couriers and shopkeepers. Their needs differ: time, cost, safety, comfort, dignity and reliability. People who do not travel on the corridor are affected too, through noise, air quality and severance (a road cutting a neighbourhood apart). In Pulatova's survey, 73.1% of respondents report no infrastructure for people with disabilities and 66.1% say pedestrian crossings are missing. The survey does not report its sample size or dates, so treat these figures with care; Week 2 returns to this question of method.

Know your data before you define the problem

  • Open data under-record what exists: 8.4 km of cycle track in OSM versus 238.7 km on the street
  • 4.79% of mahallas have no footway recorded in OSM — not recorded is not absent
  • Record source, date, coverage and limitations of every dataset (LAB1)
  • A problem defined on a wrong baseline leads to the wrong solution

20 / 26

Open data often under-record what exists on the street. OpenStreetMap shows 8.4 km of cycle track in Tashkent, but the field audit found 238.7 km. In the same way, 4.79% of mahallas have no footway recorded in OpenStreetMap, and a footway that is not recorded is not necessarily absent. If you define a problem on a wrong baseline, you will choose the wrong solution, so record the source, date, coverage and limitations of every dataset. You will build this dataset log in LAB1.

Where the city meets the region

  • Entry corridors carry commuters, intercity buses and freight into local streets
  • The M-41 corridor study used mixed methods to read traffic patterns and safety
  • If your corridor touches the ring road or an entry road, include the external flows in the boundary

21 / 26

Roads entering the city carry commuters, intercity buses and freight into local streets. A study of the M-41 corridor by Sodikov and colleagues (2026) used mixed methods to read its traffic patterns and safety. If your corridor touches the ring road or an entry road, include these external flows inside your boundary.

Discussion

For your corridor: what is the problem, for whom — and what would you see on the street if it were solved?
  • Write one sentence. Then name one effect and one cause.

22 / 26

This exercise applies the lecture to your own corridor. Write one sentence that states the problem and who it affects, without naming a solution. Then describe what you would see on the street if the problem were solved, and name one effect and one cause of the problem. If your sentence names a project, such as a new flyover, rewrite it as a problem.

Key takeaways

  • Plan for people reaching places; vehicles moved are only a means
  • A problem statement names who, where, when and how much — without a solution
  • Draw the boundary wide enough to catch displaced effects
  • Check your data before you trust your problem

23 / 26

These four points sum up how to start any corridor study. Keep the goal in mind: people reaching places, with vehicles only a means to that end. Before you propose anything, write a problem statement with no solution in it, and draw a boundary wide enough to see effects that move elsewhere. Finally, test your data, because a problem defined on a wrong baseline leads to the wrong solution.
