What you will be able to do after today

  • Name the elements of a multimodal cross-section and what each is for
  • Explain the movement and public-space functions of a street
  • Compare alternatives by person throughput, not vehicle throughput
  • Argue a reallocation of space on safety and capacity grounds

2 / 23

This lecture is about dividing a street's width between its users. You will name the elements of a multimodal cross-section, the strips of footway, lanes and planting across a street, and say what each is for. You will learn the street's two functions, movement and place. You will compare designs by how many people they move, not how many vehicles, and argue for reallocating space on safety and capacity grounds.

In a published course on urban transportation planning, the week on street design covers spatial capacity, traffic calming and the politics of street design. One of its assignments asked students to redesign Massachusetts Avenue using counts they made themselves. P7 and LAB7 follow the same logic on your Tashkent corridor. Street design is where decisions about the network, the services and safety become visible.

Two functions: movement and place

  • Movement: moving people and goods through the street
  • Place: staying, shopping, meeting, waiting, playing
  • Every street does both, in different proportions
  • GDCI: design for all users, starting with the most vulnerable
  • Classify your corridor on both axes before choosing a cross-section

4 / 23

A street has two functions. Movement means carrying people and goods through it, and place means staying, shopping, meeting, waiting and playing in it. Every street does both, in different proportions. The GDCI guide asks designers to plan for all users, starting with the most vulnerable. So place your corridor on both functions before you choose a cross-section.

Elements of a cross-section

ElementFunctionDesign question
FootwayWalking, waiting, frontage accessIs the clear path wide enough for the flow?

KerbsideParking, loading, stopsWhich use earns the kerb?

5 / 23

A cross-section is made of elements, and each has a function and a design question. The footway serves walking, waiting and access to buildings, while a planting strip or aryk gives shade, drainage and separation. Aryks are Tashkent's irrigation channels, and Week 9 returns to them. For cycle tracks, bus lanes, general lanes and the kerbside, ask questions such as whether a bus lane has enough buses per hour to justify it.

The footway is a set of zones

  • Frontage zone next to buildings: entrances, shop displays
  • Clear path: the width actually available for walking
  • Furniture and planting zone: trees, lighting, benches, signs
  • Effective width = total width minus obstacles and shy distances
  • Parked cars, kiosks and fences eat the clear path first

6 / 23

A footway has three zones. The frontage zone next to buildings holds entrances and shop displays, and the furniture and planting zone holds trees, lighting, benches and signs. Between them lies the clear path, the width actually free for walking. Effective width is the total width minus obstacles and shy distances, the gaps people keep from kerbs, walls and objects. Parked cars, kiosks and fences take space from the clear path first.

Person throughput, not vehicle throughput

  • A lane's value is the people it moves per hour, not the vehicles
  • Car lane: vehicles per hour of green × occupancy
  • Bus lane: buses per hour × passengers per bus
  • Cycle track and footway: flow per metre of usable width
  • Capacity is what a section can carry; counts show what it carries

7 / 23

A lane's value is the number of people it moves per hour, not the number of vehicles. For a car lane, multiply the vehicles per hour of green signal by car occupancy, the persons in each car. For a bus lane, multiply buses per hour by passengers per bus, and for a cycle track or footway, use the flow per metre of usable width. Capacity is what a section can carry, while counts show what it actually carries.

8 / 23

This worked example estimates the hourly person capacity of one lane of each type. A general lane passes 1,800 vehicles per hour of green, the green ratio g/C is 0.45, and each car carries 1.3 persons, so it carries about 1,050 persons per hour. A bus lane with 40 buses per hour of 50 passengers carries 2,000. A 2.0 m cycle track carries 1,500, and a 3.5 m footway carries 3,000 after 1.0 m is lost to obstacles. These are teaching assumptions, so swap them for your own counts or for values from a cited source.

When does a bus lane pay off?

  • Compare people per lane: bus lane 2,000 vs car lane about 1,050 in the example
  • With 15 buses/h of 50 passengers, the lane already carries 750 bus riders
  • A dedicated lane also buys reliability — and reliability attracts riders
  • Junction priority (Week 10) decides whether the lane's time savings survive

9 / 23

In the example, a bus lane carries 2,000 people per hour, against about 1,050 for a car lane. Even with 15 buses per hour of 50 passengers, a lane already carries 750 bus riders. A dedicated lane also makes buses more reliable, and reliability attracts more riders. Whether the lane's time savings survive depends on priority at junctions, which Week 10 covers.

Safety-led reallocation

  • Wide, multi-lane roads mean long crossings and high speeds
  • Pedestrian strikes are 57.4% of Tashkent's 2016 police crash records (August excluded)
  • Recorded death: 3.46% vs 1.08% for pedestrians vs vehicle occupants
  • Narrower lanes, refuges and shorter crossings reduce exposure and speed
  • Reallocation is a safety measure, not only a mode-shift measure

10 / 23

Wide roads with many lanes mean long crossings and high speeds. In Tashkent's 2016 police crash records, leaving out August, 57.4% of the records were pedestrian strikes. Of the recorded casualties, 3.46% of pedestrians died, against 1.08% of vehicle occupants. These figures describe people in police records, not the risk for the whole population. Narrower lanes, refuges in the middle of the road and shorter crossings reduce exposure and speed, so reallocating space is also a safety measure.

The last part of the lecture applies these ideas to Tashkent's wide streets, where different users compete for space. It uses OpenStreetMap data from October 2026, Normukhammadov's (2026) cycling thesis and Pulatova's (2024) research.

An inheritance of generous cross-sections

  • Soviet-era avenues: wide carriageways, broad sidewalks, landscaped buffers
  • Whole city, trunk to tertiary: 432.2 of 641.9 km of lane-tagged main-road carriageways have 3 or more lanes
  • 88.2% of 3,386.9 km of speed-tagged roads are signed 60 km/h
  • OSM maps only 14.3 km of dedicated busway

12 / 23

Tashkent inherited Soviet-era avenues with wide carriageways, broad sidewalks and landscaped buffers. Across the whole city, OpenStreetMap shows that 432.2 of 641.9 km of lane-tagged main-road carriageways, from trunk to tertiary roads, have three or more lanes. Of the roads with a speed tag, 88.2% of 3,386.9 km are signed 60 km/h, and 14.3 km of dedicated busway is mapped. OpenStreetMap tags are incomplete, so these numbers cover the tagged roads alone.

Three cross-sections, one width

Teaching example of a Tashkent arterial: Existing, Alt A (bus lanes + cycle tracks), Alt B (boulevard).

13 / 23

The figure draws three cross-sections of a teaching-example arterial, all with the same width between building lines. Existing has three general lanes each way and kerbside parking. Alt A turns the parking and one lane each way into a bus lane and a protected cycle track. Alt B is a boulevard: it keeps the bus lanes and cycle tracks, widens the footways and the planted median, and leaves one general lane each way.

15 / 23

The table compares the three alternatives. Walking, cycling and planting take 38% of the width in Existing, 48% in Alt A and 62% in Alt B. Car capacity falls from 6,318 to 4,212 and then 2,106 persons per hour, while total person capacity rises from 13,818 to 17,212 and then 18,706. Alt A takes kerbside parking and one lane each way, and Alt B takes two lanes each way and the parking. Capacity is not demand: a wide footway stays idle if few people walk, so add counts.

Converting sidewalks: the cycling network's hidden cost

  • 94% of the gap links that would join Tashkent's cycle paths run along existing sidewalks
  • Sidewalk conversion is cheap — but takes space from pedestrians
  • Keep the tree canopy: greener segments are shadier (ρ = −0.64)
  • Reallocate from the carriageway first where lanes exceed what junctions serve

16 / 23

Normukhammadov found that 94% of the length of the gap links, the short missing pieces that would join Tashkent's cycle paths, runs along existing sidewalks. Converting sidewalk is cheap, but it takes space from pedestrians. Keep the tree canopy as well, because greener segments are shadier: greenery and light exposure have a correlation of ρ = −0.64. Where a road has more lanes than its junctions can serve, take space from the carriageway first.

The politics of street space

  • Every metre reallocated has a loser who notices and a winner who may not
  • Shopkeepers often overestimate how many customers arrive by car
  • Pilot with paint and planters, measure, then build
  • Evidence (counts, crashes, heat) shifts the debate from opinion to trade-offs

19 / 23

Every metre given to one use is taken from another, and the loser usually notices more than the winner. Shopkeepers often overestimate how many of their customers arrive by car. A good approach is to pilot a change with paint and planters, measure the effect, and then build. Evidence on counts, crashes and heat moves the debate from opinions to trade-offs.

Discussion

Your corridor has a fixed width between building lines. Which metre would you take back from cars first — and how would you prove it was worth it?
  • Answer with a person-throughput or safety argument.

20 / 23

This question asks which metre of your corridor you would take back from cars first. The width between building lines is fixed, so any gain for one user is a loss for another. Support your choice with a person-throughput or a safety argument. Then say what evidence would show that the change was worth it.

Key takeaways

  • A street is a fixed width shared between movement and place
  • Compare alternatives by persons per hour and by safety, not vehicles per hour
  • Every reallocation has losers; state them and the evidence
  • Tashkent's wide streets make reallocation possible — protect trees and pedestrians while doing it

21 / 23

A street has a fixed width that movement and place must share. Compare alternatives by persons per hour and by safety, not by vehicles per hour. Every reallocation has losers, so name them and give your evidence. Tashkent's wide streets make reallocation possible, but protect trees and pedestrians while you do it.
