Public participation and political acceptability

LEC24 · Urban Transport Systems Planning and Design · TSTU · Prof. J. Sodikov

What you will be able to do after today

  • Choose a level of participation and be honest about it
  • Carry out a stakeholder analysis by interest and influence
  • Reach groups who rarely attend meetings
  • Separate evidence, interests and values in a consultation

2 / 23

This lecture is about involving the public in transport decisions. You will choose a level of participation and be honest about it. You will carry out a stakeholder analysis by interest and influence, and reach groups who rarely attend meetings. Finally, you will learn to separate evidence, interests and values in what people say during a consultation.

Why involve the public?

  • Legitimacy: decisions on public space need public consent
  • Knowledge: residents know where people cross, park and wait
  • Better designs: problems are found before construction
  • Acceptance: people support what they helped shape
  • Participation is a SUMP principle, not a formality

3 / 23

There are several reasons to involve the public. Decisions about public space need public consent, which gives them legitimacy. Residents know where people cross, park and wait. Their knowledge helps to find design problems before construction. People also support what they helped to shape, and the SUMP guidelines treat participation as a core principle, not a formality.

Levels of participation

Redrawn after Arnstein's ladder and the IAP2 spectrum: promise only what you will deliver.

4 / 23

The figure, redrawn after Arnstein's ladder and the IAP2 spectrum, shows five levels of participation: inform, consult, involve, collaborate and empower. From left to right, the public gains more influence on the decision. Each level carries a promise, for example that a consultation will report what changed and why. Promise only what you will deliver.

Stakeholder analysis in five steps

  • Identify everyone who affects or is affected by the project
  • Score interest: how much the project changes their situation
  • Score influence: authority, resources, ability to block or mobilise
  • Choose an engagement method for each quadrant
  • Update the matrix after every round — positions move

5 / 23

A stakeholder is anyone who affects the project or is affected by it, so start by listing them all. Score each one's interest, meaning how much the project changes their situation. Then score their influence: their authority, their resources and their power to block the project or mobilise others. Choose a way of engaging each group, and update the scores after every round, because positions move.

An interest–influence grid

Illustrative scores for the Shahrisabz St bus-lane and parking reform; LAB12 asks you to re-score them.

6 / 23

The grid places each stakeholder by interest, across, and influence, up, using illustrative scores for the Shahrisabz St bus lane and parking reform. Groups with high interest and high influence are managed closely. Groups with high interest but low influence, at the bottom right, care about the project but are hard to hear, so they are the targets for outreach. LAB12 asks you to re-score these stakeholders.

Engaging under-represented groups

  • Women, older people, children, people with disabilities and households without cars
  • Go where they are: stops, schools, clinics, markets, mahalla meetings
  • Choose times and places that suit carers and shift workers
  • Accessible formats: large print, Uzbek and Russian, oral and visual
  • Count who took part, by group, and report it

7 / 23

Some groups rarely take part in consultations: women, older people, children, people with disabilities and households without cars. Go to where they are, such as stops, schools, clinics, markets and mahalla meetings, at times that suit carers and shift workers. Use accessible formats, such as large print, Uzbek and Russian, and oral and visual material. Count who took part in each group, and report it.

Local evidence: who answers surveys?

  • In TSTU's own pedestrian survey, 72.2% of respondents were men
  • 73.1% said there is no infrastructure for people with disabilities
  • The people most affected were the least represented
  • Report the sample, and weight or supplement it before drawing conclusions

8 / 23

In the pedestrian survey from Pulatova's TSTU thesis, 72.2% of respondents were men, and the sample size is not reported. In the same survey, 73.1% said there is no infrastructure for people with disabilities. The slide's point is that the people most affected were the least represented. So report who answered, and weight or add to the sample before you draw conclusions.

The mahalla as a channel

  • Tashkent has 585 mahallas — a ready structure for local engagement
  • Mahalla meetings reach residents who never use online forms
  • But they may speak for established, older, male voices
  • Combine channels: meetings, stop-side interviews, online forms, school sessions

9 / 23

A mahalla is a neighbourhood community, and Ergashova's study analyses 585 mahallas in Tashkent. Mahallas are a ready structure for local engagement, and their meetings reach residents who never use online forms. But they may speak mainly for established, older, male voices. So combine several channels, such as meetings, interviews at stops, online forms and sessions at schools.

Evidence, interests and values

Statement heardTypeHow to respond
"The bus lane will create queues"Claim about evidenceShow the junction calculation; offer to monitor
"My customers come by car"Interest (and a testable claim)Survey how customers arrive; protect loading
"Streets are for cars"ValueAcknowledge; explain the city's objectives
"Older people cannot cross in time"Evidence and valueChange the pedestrian timing; report back

10 / 23

The table sorts statements heard in a consultation into three types. A claim about evidence, such as "The bus lane will create queues", can be tested, so answer it with the junction calculation and an offer to monitor. An interest, such as "My customers come by car", is answered with design, for example by surveying how customers arrive and protecting loading. A value, such as "Streets are for cars", is acknowledged and answered with the city's objectives.

Managing conflict

  • Separate positions (what people demand) from interests (why)
  • Joint fact-finding: agree what to measure before arguing about results
  • Trials and pilots: temporary schemes let people judge real effects
  • Mitigate concentrated losses: loading bays, phased works, access for shops
  • Publish what changed because of the consultation

11 / 23

A position is what people demand, and an interest is why they demand it, so separate the two. In joint fact-finding, everyone agrees what to measure before arguing about the results. Trials and pilots let people judge the real effects of a temporary scheme. Reduce losses that fall on a few people, for example with loading bays, phased works and access for shops, and publish what changed because of the consultation.

Political acceptability

  • Acceptance depends on visible benefits, perceived fairness and trust in enforcement
  • Losses are felt more strongly than equal gains
  • How revenue is used matters: parking charges spent on the street are easier to accept
  • Acceptability can change after people experience a scheme — design trials to show it

12 / 23

People accept a scheme more readily when the benefits are visible, the scheme seems fair and they trust the enforcement. Losses are felt more strongly than gains of the same size. How revenue is used also matters: parking charges spent on the same street are easier to accept. Acceptance can change once people have experienced a scheme, so design trials that can show it.

A consultation programme for a bus lane

Illustrative sequence: map, listen, present options with evidence, consult, respond, decide, trial, review.

13 / 23

The figure gives an illustrative sequence for consulting on a bus lane. It starts with stakeholder mapping and a pre-consultation about problems, not solutions. Options are then presented with evidence, followed by a formal consultation and a response report, "you said, we did". After the decision comes a trial, then a review and the permanent scheme. You will use this sequence in the P12 role-play and in your project's participation plan.

Who spoke — and who did not

The charts show who wrote the synthetic comments. Women wrote 27.5% of them and people aged 60 and over wrote 11.9%. Car commuters and shop owners wrote 40.6%, but bus passengers only 11.9%. The loudest objection, traffic congestion with 18 objections, came mostly from car commuters. Before drawing conclusions, compare the respondents with the corridor population.

From comments to a response register

  • Group comments by theme; count support, conditional and opposition
  • For each theme decide: accept, modify, explain or reject
  • Record the project change or the reason, an owner and a date
  • Publish a 'you said, we did' report before the decision
  • Never record names, phone numbers or addresses

18 / 23

A response register records how the project answers each theme of the comments. Group the comments by theme and count support, conditional support and opposition. For each theme, decide whether to accept, modify, explain or reject, and record the change or the reason, an owner and a date. Publish a "you said, we did" report before the decision, and never record names, phone numbers or addresses.

Discussion

The loudest objections come from drivers; the biggest gains go to bus passengers who did not attend. What do you do?
  • Two minutes in pairs: one engagement action and one design change.

19 / 23

In this case, drivers make the loudest objections, but bus passengers who did not attend gain the most. Decide what you would do about this imbalance. Propose one action to hear the missing group and one change to the design.

Key takeaways

  • Say which level of participation you offer, and keep the promise
  • Interest–influence analysis shows who to involve and who is unheard
  • Count who took part before reading what they said
  • Answer evidence with evidence, interests with design, values with objectives

20 / 23

Say which level of participation you offer, and keep that promise. An interest–influence analysis shows whom to involve and who is not being heard. Count who took part before you read what they said. Answer evidence with evidence, interests with design and values with the city's objectives.
